    GDPR Compliance Analysis

    Ad Omnia unifies emergency data from Civil Protection, INEM and the security forces. Most of that data is about places, vehicles and events, but some of it is about people: the operators who use the platform, the crews of the vehicles on the map and, above all, the citizens involved in an incident. This page maps every piece of personal data in the platform to the law that governs it, shows how the current design complies and lists what still has to be done.

    Scope: private app, public app, AI engine, plugins, this microsite Based on: requirements, data model, architecture, Ad-Omnia/infra/global_db/init/init.sql Last reviewed: 29/10/2026

    At a glance

    Covered by design

    Public app collects no personal data

    Citizens use it without an account and only see validated incidents at an approximate location (NFR-SEC-3, FR-PUB-3, FR-PUB-7). Art. 5(1)(c) GDPR

    Covered by design

    A human decides, not the AI

    Recommendations are applied only after explicit operator confirmation (FR-REC-3), so no decision is based solely on automated processing. Art. 22 GDPR

    Before deployment

    Health and criminal data in real feeds

    INEM and police data can include special categories and offence data. A DPIA is mandatory before going live. Art. 9 Art. 10 Art. 35 GDPR

    Before deployment

    Immutable audit log vs. right to erasure

    Resolved by the legal-obligation exception, as long as the log stores IDs and not personal details. Art. 17(3)(b) GDPR

    Before deployment

    Dispatch AI is "high-risk" under the AI Act

    Resource dispatch recommendations fall under Annex III, point 5(d). Obligations apply from 2 December 2027. Annex III AI Act

    Fix now

    This microsite leaks visitor IPs to Google

    Fonts are loaded from fonts.googleapis.com, and the site has no privacy notice. Both are quick fixes. Art. 6(1) GDPR Art. 13 GDPR

    • Citation chips like Art. 5 GDPR open the legal text in a new tab. GDPR articles link to an article-by-article reader; the official texts are listed in References.
    • Requirement IDs (FR-…, NFR-…) point to the requirements page, so every claim can be traced to the design.
    • This is an engineering analysis written by students, not legal advice. It should be validated with the University of Aveiro's Data Protection Officer (epd@ua.pt) and, for a real deployment, with each agency's DPO.

    1. The legal framework

    Data protection is a fundamental right in the EU Art. 8 Charter Art. 16(1) TFEU and in Portugal Art. 35 CRP. These are the instruments that apply to Ad Omnia, from most to least central:

    EU regulation · core

    GDPR — Regulation (EU) 2016/679

    Applies directly in every Member State since 25 May 2018, without transposition. Governs all personal data processed by the private app, the public app and the microsite.

    PT law · core

    Lei n.º 58/2019

    Executes the GDPR in Portugal: names the CNPD as supervisory authority and fills the GDPR's open clauses (DPOs in public bodies, retention, employment, health data, research, fines on public entities).

    EU directive + PT law · police data

    Directive (EU) 2016/680 · Lei n.º 59/2019

    The GDPR does not cover processing by competent authorities to prevent, investigate or prosecute crime Art. 2(2)(d) GDPR. If PSP/GNR use the platform for those purposes, this regime applies instead.

    EU regulation · AI

    AI Act — Regulation (EU) 2024/1689

    Classifies AI used to dispatch or prioritise police, firefighters and medical aid as high-risk. Amended by the Digital Omnibus, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, which moved the Annex III deadline to 2 December 2027.

    EU directive + PT law · security

    NIS2 — Directive (EU) 2022/2555 · Decreto-Lei n.º 125/2025

    Cybersecurity regime for essential and important entities, including public administration. Agencies running the platform must notify significant incidents within 24 hours, alongside any GDPR breach notification.

    EU directive + PT law · websites

    ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC · Lei n.º 41/2004

    Storing or reading anything on a visitor's device (cookies, localStorage) needs consent unless it is strictly necessary for a service the visitor asked for Art. 5(3) ePrivacy Art. 5 Lei 41/2004.

    PT law · sector

    Lei n.º 27/2006 — Lei de Bases da Proteção Civil

    Defines civil protection as a public task. This is the national law that gives agencies their lawful basis for processing operational data Art. 6(1)(e) and 6(3) GDPR.

    CNPD regulation · DPIA list

    CNPD Regulamento n.º 798/2018

    The Portuguese list of processing operations that always require a DPIA Art. 35(4) GDPR. Several items match Ad Omnia (see § 8).

    2. Who is responsible?

    The GDPR assigns obligations by role. The controller decides why and how data is processed Art. 4(7) GDPR; a processor processes it on the controller's behalf Art. 4(8) GDPR, under a written contract Art. 28 GDPR EDPB Guidelines 07/2020. Who is who depends on the stage of the project:

    Who Role Why
    The five team members Controllers of anything they process for the project The UA DPO's guidance on student work: a student processing data to pursue their studies decides the purpose and means, so the student is the controller, not the University (scenario 2 in the UA DPO training slides).
    University of Aveiro Controller only of deliverables submitted for assessment, from the moment of submission Scenario 3 in the same slides: evaluating submitted work is a University function.
    Advisors Not controllers They advise and grade; the slides note supervisors generally are not controllers, and being both would conflict with their role as evaluators.
    GitHub (repo + Pages hosting) Processor / independent provider Hosts code and serves the microsite; logs visitor IP addresses.

    Consequence: the prototype must not use real personal data from emergency operations. It runs on public open data (e.g. Fogos.pt), synthetic data from the simulation module and hand-made test records. With that rule, the only real personal data in the project today is the team's own data on this microsite (see § 10).

    Who Role Why
    Each agency (ANEPC, INEM, PSP, GNR, municipal civil protection) Controller of the data it brings in and the actions of its control room It decides the purposes (emergency response) under its own legal mandate. The private app is installed on-premises in each control room.
    Agencies sharing one COP Possibly joint controllers If they jointly decide what is shared and how, they need an arrangement setting out who answers data subjects Art. 26 GDPR.
    Ad Omnia (as a supplier) Processor, only when it supports, maintains or hosts It acts on documented instructions and needs an Art. 28 contract covering confidentiality, security, sub-processors, deletion at the end of the contract and audits Art. 28(3) GDPR.
    Plugin and data-source providers Independent controllers of their own sources; processors only if they process agency data A public API such as Fogos.pt is a source, not a processor. A hosted plugin that receives agency data would be a sub-processor.
    PSP/GNR acting for crime prevention or investigation Competent authorities under Lei n.º 59/2019 That processing falls outside the GDPR Art. 2(2)(d) GDPR; access to it must stay separated from the GDPR-governed COP data.

    3. What personal data does Ad Omnia touch?

    Personal data is any information about an identified or identifiable person, directly or indirectly, including location data and online identifiers Art. 4(1) GDPR. That is broader than it looks: a station ID that can be matched to the operator on shift, an incident address or a vehicle position can all identify someone. Data that is truly anonymous is outside the GDPR Recital 26 GDPR.

    Special category health, or other Art. 9 data Personal identifies or can identify someone Not personal aggregated, synthetic or about objects

    The single biggest lever: keep free text out of the core

    Almost every special-category risk enters through three free-text or JSON fields: incident.description, orders.content and the source_specific_* columns. Connector plugins should map source data to structured, non-identifying fields (type, severity, location, counts) and drop names, phone numbers and clinical notes at ingestion. This is data minimisation applied at the boundary Art. 5(1)(c) GDPR and data protection by design Art. 25(1) GDPR.

    4. The seven principles

    Every processing operation has to respect the principles of Art. 5 GDPR, and the controller must be able to demonstrate it Art. 5(2) GDPR.

    Principle What it asks How Ad Omnia complies Status
    Lawfulness, fairness, transparency 5(1)(a) A legal basis, and people are told what happens to their data Legal bases mapped in § 5. Transparency is the gap: no privacy notice exists yet for the public app or for operators Before deployment
    Purpose limitation 5(1)(b) Collected for explicit purposes, not reused for incompatible ones Each entity in the data model records its rationale (user story). Simulations never write to live tables (FR-PRD-2). Reusing operational data to train AI is a further purpose: needs the compatibility test of Art. 6(4) or the research route of Art. 89 Before deployment
    Data minimisation 5(1)(c) Only what is needed Public app: zero personal identifiers (NFR-SEC-3), approximate location (FR-PUB-3), no operational fields (FR-PUB-7). Stations, not individuals, hold licenses. Free-text fields still need filtering (tip above) Covered by design
    Accuracy 5(1)(d) Correct and up to date Per-source trust level and staleness threshold; outdated sources are flagged (FR-ING-2, NFR-AVL-1); duplicates are merged, not lost (Incident Relation) Covered by design
    Storage limitation 5(1)(e) Kept no longer than needed No retention period is defined yet for incidents, observations, orders or logs. In Portugal the period is the one set by law, otherwise what the purpose requires, then data must be destroyed or anonymised Art. 21 Lei 58/2019 Before deployment
    Integrity and confidentiality 5(1)(f) Protected against unauthorised access, loss and damage One-way private→public flow (NFR-SEC-2), licenses stored with slow salted hashes (NFR-SEC-4), tamper-evident records (NFR-INT-1), sandboxed plugins on an internal-only network. Details in § 7 Covered by design
    Accountability 5(2) Prove all of the above Immutable audit log of every change and export (FR-AUD-1, FR-AUD-2); this analysis; a draft record of processing (§ 8) Covered by design

    5. Lawful basis

    Processing is lawful only if one of six bases applies Art. 6(1) GDPR. Consent is the wrong tool here: a person in an emergency cannot give free consent, and public authorities cannot rely on legitimate interests when performing their tasks Art. 6(1), last subparagraph. Instead:

    1. Operational data in the private app: public task. Agencies process incident and resource data to perform a task in the public interest laid down by national law Art. 6(1)(e) and 6(3) GDPR, e.g. civil protection under Lei n.º 27/2006. Where someone's life is at stake, vital interests also apply Art. 6(1)(d) GDPR; the GDPR itself mentions natural and man-made disasters as an example Recital 46 GDPR.
    2. Health data (INEM): one of the Art. 9 exceptions. Health data is prohibited by default Art. 9(1) GDPR. The relevant exceptions are vital interests of a person unable to consent 9(2)(c), emergency medical care under professional secrecy 9(2)(h) and 9(3) and public health 9(2)(i). In Portugal, access follows need-to-know, by bound professionals, electronically, with access logs Art. 29 Lei 58/2019.
    3. Offence data (police feeds): only under official authority. Data relating to criminal offences may be processed only under the control of official authority or when authorised by law Art. 10 GDPR. Investigative use goes through Lei n.º 59/2019.
    4. Operators and crews: employment context. Data about staff is processed within labour law Art. 28 Lei 58/2019. Vehicle positions show where resources are; they must not be used to evaluate crews, or the processing becomes employee location tracking that requires a DPIA (CNPD list, item 3).
    5. Public app logs: public task, narrowly. Short-lived IP logs to keep the service secure are part of running the public information service Art. 6(1)(e) GDPR.
    6. AI training: further processing with safeguards. Use synthetic data first; then pseudonymised historical data with the safeguards of Art. 89(1) GDPR and Art. 31 Lei 58/2019 (minimisation, anonymisation or pseudonymisation where the aim allows).

    Not applicable: consent and children's consent

    Consent Art. 6(1)(a) and the age-13 rule for information society services Art. 16 Lei 58/2019 do not apply: the public app has no accounts and processes no personal data of its users beyond security logs, and the private app runs on public-task bases. Not applicable

    6. Rights of the people in the data

    Each right in Arts. 12–22 GDPR either works normally, is limited by law, or does not apply to a public-task basis. The platform must support the first group and document the rest.

    Right Article How it works in Ad Omnia Status
    Information 13 / 14 Operators: notice at onboarding. Incident subjects: data is collected indirectly in an emergency; individual notice is impossible or disproportionate, so a public notice per agency is used Art. 14(5)(b) GDPR. Public app: short notice about logs Before deployment
    Access 15 Historical search by incident and the per-incident audit trail (FR-HIST-1, FR-AUD-1) let an agency find everything about a request. Limited where a legal duty of secrecy applies Art. 20 Lei 58/2019 Covered by design
    Rectification 16 Incidents and resources are editable (FR-INC-1); every correction is itself logged Covered by design
    Erasure 17 Does not apply while processing is needed for a legal obligation or public task 17(3)(b) or for legal claims 17(3)(e). Free-text personal details must be erasable once the retention period ends — see the spotlight below Before deployment
    Restriction 18 Needs a "restricted" flag that hides a record from operational views without deleting it Before deployment
    Portability 20 Does not apply to processing necessary for a public task Art. 20(3) GDPR Not applicable
    Objection 21 Applies to public-task processing, but the agency can show compelling grounds (emergency response) Art. 21(1) GDPR Covered by design
    No solely automated decisions 22 Predictions inform; recommendations need explicit confirmation, stored with an optional reason (FR-REC-3). Operators see a justification and a confidence level (FR-REC-1, FR-PRD-1) Covered by design

    Member States may restrict these rights by law for public security, defence or the prevention of crime, provided the restriction is necessary and proportionate Art. 23 GDPR.

    Spotlight · design tension

    Immutable audit log vs. the right to erasure

    The conflict. NFR-INT-1 says no audit, order or decision record can ever be edited or deleted, and init.sql enforces it with a trigger. A person can ask for their data to be erased Art. 17(1) GDPR.

    Why it is lawful. Erasure does not apply when keeping the data is necessary to comply with a legal obligation, to perform a public task, or to defend legal claims Art. 17(3)(b) and (e) GDPR. Emergency decision logs are exactly that kind of record.

    What the design must still do.

    1. Store references, not personal details, in audit_log.details: entity IDs and changed field names, never the text of a description or an order.
    2. Keep personal free text only in the operational tables, where it can be erased or anonymised when its retention period ends Art. 21 Lei 58/2019. The audit entry stays valid; it just points to a record that is now anonymised.
    3. Identify operators by station ID, which is pseudonymous on its own Art. 4(5) GDPR; the station-to-person mapping (shift rosters) stays in the agency's own HR systems, outside Ad Omnia.

    7. Security of processing

    The controller and processor must implement measures appropriate to the risk, including pseudonymisation and encryption, confidentiality, availability and resilience, and regular testing Art. 32 GDPR.     8. Accountability paperwork

    Data Protection Impact Assessment

    A DPIA is required when processing is likely to result in a high risk Art. 35(1) GDPR. For a real deployment of Ad Omnia it is mandatory, because several triggers match:

    • Large-scale processing of special categories (health data from INEM) — Art. 35(3)(b) GDPR
    • Electronic transmission of health data — CNPD list, item 1
    • Art. 9/10 data processed with new technology (predictive AI) — CNPD list, item 6(a)
    • Interconnection of personal data from several agencies into one picture — CNPD list, item 6(c)
    • Tracking employees' location to evaluate them — avoided by design, as long as vehicle positions are never used to rate crews (CNPD list, item 3)

    If the DPIA shows a high residual risk, the controller must consult the CNPD before starting Art. 36 GDPR. For the prototype, which uses no real personal data, a DPIA is not mandatory, but the UA DPO's guidance is to do a prior risk analysis for any new processing; this page is that first pass.

    Record of processing activities

    Each controller must keep a record of its processing Art. 30(1) GDPR. Draft entries an agency can adopt:

    Field (Art. 30(1)) Private app: operational picture Public app: citizen information AI engine: model training
    Purpose Coordinate emergency response Inform citizens of active incidents and warnings Forecast incident progression and risk
    Data subjects Operators, crews, persons involved in incidents Visitors (IP only) Persons in historical incidents
    Data categories Station IDs, decisions, incident and resource data, possible health/offence data IP address, timestamp, requested URL Pseudonymised incident features, locations generalised to a grid
    Recipients Other agencies in the COP; public app (validated, non-personal incidents only) None None (models only)
    Transfers outside the EU None (on-premises) None if hosted in the EU None
    Retention (proposal, to be fixed by each agency) Legal period for operational records; free text anonymised after incident closure + review period 30 days Until model retirement; raw data deleted after feature extraction
    Security measures § 7 § 7, log rotation Pseudonymisation, access limited to the AI service

    Data Protection Officer

    Public authorities must designate a DPO Art. 37(1)(a) GDPR Art. 12 Lei 58/2019. In Portugal the DPO also runs periodic and unannounced audits and raises staff awareness of security incidents Art. 11 Lei 58/2019. Every agency already has one; Ad Omnia's job is to make their work easy: exportable audit trails (FR-AUD-1) and this documentation. Not required for the team

    Transfers outside the EU

    Personal data may leave the EU only under an adequacy decision or appropriate safeguards Arts. 44–46 GDPR. The private app is on-premises and the public app should be hosted in the EU, so no transfer is needed. GitHub (repository and microsite) is a US provider: transfers to US companies certified under the EU–US Data Privacy Framework rely on the Commission's adequacy decision Decision (EU) 2023/1795, upheld by the General Court in Latombe v Commission (T-553/23, 3 September 2025). The team should confirm GitHub's certification and keep emergency data out of the repository.

    9. AI and anonymisation

    Anonymised or only pseudonymised?

    Anonymised data is outside the GDPR; pseudonymised data is still personal data Art. 4(5) GDPR Recital 26 GDPR. The difference matters for training data, because re-identification is easier than it seems: postcode, date of birth and sex alone re-identify most people, and the Netflix Prize dataset was de-anonymised by matching a handful of ratings with public IMDb profiles (both examples from the UA DPO training slides; see also WP29 Opinion 05/2014). Incident locations are the equivalent quasi-identifier here: an exact point often is a home address.

    1. Prefer synthetic data. The simulation module already generates synthetic incidents (Simulated Incident), fully isolated from live data.
    2. Generalise locations. Snap historical incident points to a grid or parish level before training; risk ratings are already per zone.
    3. Drop free text. Train on structured features (type, severity, time, weather, zone), never on descriptions.
    4. Destroy the raw extract once features are built, so the training set can be treated as anonymous; until then it is personal data.

    The AI Act

    Question Answer Citation
    Is the prototype covered? No. Research, testing and development before placing on the market or putting into service is excluded Art. 2(8) AI Act
    Would the deployed recommendation engine be high-risk? Yes. AI used to dispatch, or set priority in the dispatch of, police, firefighters and medical aid is listed Annex III, 5(d), Art. 6(2)
    From when? 2 December 2027 for Annex III systems, after the Digital Omnibus Regulation (EU) 2026/1744
    What would the provider have to show? Risk management, data governance for training data, automatic logging, transparency to deployers, human oversight Arts. 9, 10, 12, 13, 14
    What would agencies have to do? Use it as instructed, assign human oversight, keep logs; public bodies run a fundamental rights impact assessment first Art. 26, Art. 27

    The current requirements already point in the right direction: confirmation before action (FR-REC-3), a justification per recommendation (FR-REC-1), confidence, generation time and input data for every prediction (FR-PRD-1), and the engine failing without taking the platform down (NFR-AVL-2).

    Glossary

    Personal data — any information about an identified or identifiable natural person Art. 4(1).
    Processing — anything done with personal data: collecting, storing, consulting, combining, erasing Art. 4(2).
    Pseudonymisation — data that can no longer be attributed to a person without extra information kept separately; still personal data Art. 4(5).
    Anonymisation — data that no longer relates to an identifiable person; outside the GDPR Recital 26.
    Controller / processor — who decides purposes and means / who processes on the controller's behalf Art. 4(7)–(8).
    Special categories — health, genetic, biometric data, racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious beliefs, trade-union membership, sex life or sexual orientation Art. 9(1).
    Personal data breach — accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, disclosure of or access to personal data Art. 4(12).
    DPIA — Data Protection Impact Assessment Art. 35.
    CNPD — Comissão Nacional de Proteção de Dados, the Portuguese supervisory authority Art. 3 Lei 58/2019.
